# Digest — Sep 2–15, 2026

**TL;DR:** 13 calls. Top theme: how a tokenless ordering layer establishes trust and governance; close second: whether the fellowship's funding and timeline work for academics.

Period: Sep 2–15, 2026 · Calls covered: 13
This digest covers 13 external calls with a summary available (14 recorded, 1 with no transcript) held Sep 2 to Sep 15, 2026, with professors, researchers, an IBM researcher and two student blockchain clubs. The biggest recurring theme: how a tokenless ordering layer establishes trust and governance, followed closely by whether the fellowship's funding and timeline work for academics.
## 1. Trust model, consensus and ordering without tokens
- David Starobinski (Boston University) pushed hardest on the trust model: "there is still going to be some root of trust there, right?" and "if you want to do something without tokens, I don't see another solution than having some nodes that will in some way ... [be] your trust". He suggested a trusted, NTP-like time service with cryptographic proof, while noting time cannot be perfectly synchronized and resolution may be probabilistic.
- David Starobinski (BU) also raised governance and centralization in maintaining the common software and protocols ("someone needs to decide at the end what change are being implemented") and the difficulty of avoiding forks ("It's not known how to address it.").
- Yunwen Liu (KU Leuven) asked whether the events are real-world events and why plain timestamping would not be enough. The answer given on the call was that there is no global clock, so a heartbeat is needed to bound when something was anchored.
- Roozbeh Sarenche (KU Leuven) called the project very ambitious and questioned whether it is trying to solve all the fundamental problems in blockchain. He pushed back that decentralization may be impossible at this scope, while acknowledging ambitious rather than impossible.
- Nitin Singh (IBM Research) described the chain as a history or "truth" artifact and asked whether OV is an anchor chain (answer given: an Internet service, not necessarily a chain). He raised centralized sequencers and front-running, and said data availability, encoding and storage "get messy". On interoperability he noted many people try to solve it by standardization (including IETF efforts on asset and message formats) and questioned whether that is enough.
- Adam Rusch (UIUC) reacted positively to separating the coordination layer from execution ("I love what you're saying is separating out that coordination layer", "why reinvent what everyone else does"). He asked: if you avoid a tokenized system, what is the incentive to contribute, and is there an alternative measure of each participant's relative contribution?
- Nikita Borisov (UIUC) found the ordering-first approach interesting and close to his work on privacy-preserving logs and proofs. He explored decoupling ordering from execution, interoperability and avoiding Balkanization across blockchains, and asked how well defined OV is: "is there a manifesto or something that describes it?" and whether there are concrete milestones ("you need to walk before you can run").
- Nikita Borisov (UIUC) also discussed gaps in certificate-transparency verification and auditing, as a comparable system where transparency monitoring "doesn't work in the current state of things".
## 2. Post-quantum cryptography and proof systems
- Nitin Singh (IBM Research) confirmed the core open problem: STARK proofs are post-quantum secure but too large, and on-chain verification can be prohibitive. He called sub-10KB, post-quantum, transparent and fast-verifiable proofs the "holy grail", and cited a 25KB transparent (not post-quantum) result and Dory-style improvements as the current frontier.
- Carmit Hazay (Ligero, Bar-Ilan University) described Ligero's post-quantum, compliance-composable privacy and anonymity product that works with any chain and wallet. She said the tools are not yet deployed at scale.
- Huaxiong Wang (NTU) said the initiative is "quite related" to his research, which covers security, multiparty secret schemes and more recently zero-knowledge cryptography.
## 3. Agent identity, privacy and use cases
- Nikita Borisov (UIUC), after a year as a visiting researcher at Cloudflare, said the bot-versus-human line "is getting blurred and is going to go away soon", so "what happens to identity?" is "the real question that ... is on my mind". He works on anonymous tokens for private browsing and noted "there's a fundamental tension between them doing that and the fact that people don't always want to be tracked".
- Roozbeh Sarenche (KU Leuven) raised a direct concern that anchoring could create major privacy issues for the underlying users.
- Carmit Hazay (Ligero) said institutions need privacy but fear compliance problems after Tornado Cash, so many stopped using privacy tools. She argued blockchains are not dead: stablecoins are already used for salaries and banks are looking at the infrastructure.
- Nitin Singh (IBM) pointed to verifiability for AI (for example questions about whether an agent used particular data) as a classic use case, and asked whether the target use cases are fast atomic asset exchange, bonds and cryptographically encoded escrow or agreements.
- Arnav Chandak (Illini Blockchain, UIUC) asked whether OV is similar to Coinbase's x402 system (agent service discovery and machine-to-machine payments over HTTP), and whether agents would interact with the ordering layer directly or through intermediary protocols.
## 4. Legal and governance questions
- Rolf Weber (University of Zurich) said the paper has "kaum einen rechtlichen Anker" (barely any legal anchor). He is a law professor and practicing attorney, not a technologist, but confirmed the legal questions are in his field (internet law, blockchain, EU/US stablecoin regulation). He noted international guidelines already exist for the legal qualification of AI agents.
- Adam Rusch (UIUC), who helped write the Cardano constitution and sits on its constitutional committee and the Intersect board, asked how the association, the live network and the different roles will be governed, and whether chargebacks imply a refereeing or arbitration layer.
## 5. Formal verification and AI-assisted tooling
- Michael Tautschnig (formerly AWS verification, independent) said proofs are usually a tool rather than the product customers pay for, and questioned whether customers will pay directly for proofs. He sees code transformation with correctness guarantees (for example Java to Rust) as a more promising product. He said generative AI has transformed specification generation and tool development (he runs about 10 agents at a time), though correctness still has to be checked. He expressed some surprise at the two to three year horizon and said his skills are most relevant at the implementation stage.
- Lior Rotem (Hebrew University) noted the verification researchers there do not yet work on this area, so outreach to them would mainly build awareness.
## 6. Fellowship design, funding and adoption
- David Starobinski (BU) flagged university contracting and IP as a major roadblock: gift versus contract, and that deliverables, timelines and strings trigger overhead and bureaucracy. He warned universities ramp up slowly because students must be recruited, hired and trained, said one PhD student per university is slow, and suggested company-university teams or a few consulted experts for fast DARPA-like efforts. He described a model where a company allocated a pool to BU and BU ran an internal call for proposals with the company's reviewers having the final word. He asked for documentation for BU's industry relations office and invited Andreas to the "Research on Tap" workshop at BU in early November.
- Charalampos Papamanthou (Yale) said "it's going to be hard to support a team ... with a hundred thousand per year" (he was told the figures are indicative). He asked for more concrete guidance on the two-pager and whether the call for proposals is fixed. He offered a clean-slate approach to "take this new problems head on" and said he will work on a two-pager with Yale colleagues.
- Clara Schneidewind and Pedro Moreno-Sanchez (MPI-SP) said both are changing academic positions, so time capacity is limited and the commitment is hard to meet in the first iteration. They worry that integrating students is not aligned with what students need to succeed in academia, that postdocs will struggle to see the time-to-outcome ratio, and that a small initial commitment could grow into a large effort with perhaps only $25k reaching an individual. Pedro asked how the program differs from a conventional NSF grant and what concrete, timely deliverables are expected. They agreed to share an intro email with interested collaborators.
- Adam Rusch (UIUC) said university administration makes a fall start unrealistic and spring more likely. On publications he said "six months can be an optimistic timeline for getting a paper in a journal", and accepted that prototypes and papers are "an and not an or".
- Nikita Borisov (UIUC) saw parallels between OV's approach and DARPA (high risk, higher reward, focus on deliverables) versus typical NSF funding of "half a million dollars for three years". He wants to understand how OV plans to engage academia.
- Lior Rotem (Hebrew University) said researchers in Israel are motivated by intellectual interest and funding, and that funding is less of a constraint there than in the US. Since faculty receive funding announcements daily, he favored targeted outreach first. He named Hebrew University researchers in cryptography, blockchain security, distributed computing and incentives, and agreed to introduce collaborators at other Israeli institutions.
- Yunwen Liu and Roozbeh Sarenche (KU Leuven) had planned a smaller one-year project with one master's student and will discuss aligning it with OV's broader requirements.
- Interest and offers: Rolf Weber (UZH) would consider a short pre-proposal with UZH colleagues, though he cannot decide quickly given the unpaid effort ("Das kann ich jetzt heute Abend nicht entscheiden"). Huaxiong Wang (NTU) asked what the collaboration model is and will share the materials with NTU colleagues. Carmit Hazay (Ligero) will explain the initiative to her co-founder. Michael Tautschnig and Nitin Singh (IBM) will share the materials with colleagues, Michael asking for written eligibility criteria. Arnav Chandak offered to host events linking Illini Blockchain with the Illinois professors and co-host awareness events with the AI Alignment Club.
## Open questions for R&D
1. Without tokens, what is the root of trust, and which nodes carry it? (David Starobinski)
1. Who decides protocol and software changes without centralizing, and how are forks avoided? (David Starobinski, Adam Rusch on governance)
1. What replaces token incentives, and how is each participant's relative contribution measured? (Adam Rusch)
1. Why is timestamping not sufficient, and what clock or heartbeat assumptions does ordering rely on? (Yunwen Liu)
1. Is full decentralization feasible at this scope, and what privacy exposure does anchoring create for underlying users? (Roozbeh Sarenche)
1. Is OV an anchor chain, how are centralized sequencers and front-running avoided, and can cross-chain interoperability be solved by standardization? (Nitin Singh)
1. Can a post-quantum, transparent, fast-verifiable proof under about 10KB be built? (Nitin Singh)
1. What happens to identity as the line between bots and people blurs, given the tension with users not wanting to be tracked? (Nikita Borisov)
1. Do agents use the ordering layer directly or through intermediary protocols, and how does OV differ from x402? (Arnav Chandak)
1. Do chargebacks imply an arbitration layer? (Adam Rusch)
1. What is the legal anchor for node operators and AI agents? (Rolf Weber)
1. Where is the product value of formal proofs compared with verified code transformation? (Michael Tautschnig)
1. Is there a concise manifesto and milestone path for OV? (Nikita Borisov)
