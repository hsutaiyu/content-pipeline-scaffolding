# Digest — Jul 22–Aug 4, 2026

**TL;DR:** 10 calls. Biggest recurring theme: why a neutral ordering layer is needed now and what the first adoption wave looks like (5 people); identity, attribution and authorization came up in 4 calls.

Period: Jul 22–Aug 4, 2026 · Calls covered: 10
This digest covers 10 external calls with pipeline contacts between Jul 22 and Aug 4, 2026 (dates by recording storedAt, UTC). The biggest recurring theme: several people (Alan Chan, Dazza Greenwood, Veeraj Chimanpure, Kartik Nayak, Lynn LoPucki) independently asked why the neutral ordering layer is needed now and what the first adoption wave looks like. Identity, attribution and authorization came up in four calls. Note that long transcripts were extracted in pieces, so some mid-call content may be missing.
## 1. Why this layer, and why not existing systems or direct communication
- Lynn LoPucki (Univ. of Florida Law) pushed back repeatedly: "why does somebody want this bulletin board instead of communicating directly with the people that they want to communicate with?" He said the explanation lost him ("You've lost me entirely") and described a recent bank transfer done on Zoom with credit union and bank staff asking identification questions: "Why do I need some bulletin board?"
- Kartik Nayak (Duke) asked why existing blockchains cannot do this: "I think if anything, I think they give you more than what I guess one needs." He asked whether they could simply serve as a data store.
- Veeraj Chimanpure (UMN blockchain club) asked "is there something else like this already in the blockchain world?" His framing: every system is almost its own internet, with no central root source of truth.
- Charalampos Papamanthou (Yale) noted that for public blockchains you can already query individual chains and use their roots of trust; this is not possible for private payment or economic systems. He observed coordination and computation are already decoupled inside individual blockchains, so OV is "decoupling that across economic systems." He also asked whether OV is a startup or a foundation, and confirmed the goal is to support research.
- Dazza Greenwood (CIVICS.com, moving to Stanford) said the draft rests on many compounded bets: "a lot of bets that are decreasing probability when you start compounding them" to conclude a neutral coordination layer with a timeline is what belongs at the bottom. He said much of this is already handled domain by domain, "But it's not missing per se. It might be missing in the future as we use agents." Earlier in the call he said "the idea of having a neutral kind of ordering layer at the bottom does sort of make a certain amount of sense to me."
- Sanjam Garg (UC Berkeley) asked what "neutral economic coordination" means, then offered that it removes technical overhead of building bridges between systems, and that "bridges is one application" and OV could perhaps do more.
## 2. Adoption path and first use cases
- Alan Chan (GovAI) said he was most interested in adoption: how any resulting technology gets widely adopted, how much OV must work with existing agent providers, developers and big platforms, and which niches adopt first. He asked for use cases in the next one to three years.
- Alan Chan was most bullish on near-term enterprise adoption, specifically security inside AI companies using agents for R&D and business processes. He asked whether enterprise requirements would shape the academic layer and whether enterprises had confirmed the research needs. He said there did not yet seem to be strong demand for secure cross-enterprise economic coordination, and many enterprises are not thinking about it.
- Dazza Greenwood said transaction ordering would not be "the initial blocker, nor will it be the initial resource that causes adoption." He urged mapping the first adoption wave that makes a second wave possible, working toward a multilayer architecture. Near-term gaps he named: good attribution from an agent back to its parties, and a durable, externally legible (ideally verifiable) statement of what authorization the agent has to act for its principal.
- Veeraj Chimanpure asked whether third-party adoption would be required and how it would be executed.
- Prashant Pilla (UMN) asked whether other organizations are already solving this or whether OV aims to consolidate efforts.
## 3. Identity, attribution, authorization and provenance
- Lynn LoPucki argued corporate registration tells you "virtually nothing about the human beings associated with the entity" ("Somebody sent $70 to the Secretary of state somewhere, and now there's a corporation"), and that anyone can download or fabricate documentation. He pointed to his own human identification theory and identity theft work as the most relevant to OV.
- Lynn LoPucki on proofs: "You don't have to have money in a dead tree stump to send a picture of you putting money in a dead tree stump." He questioned how any electronic proof ties to the real economic artifact.
- Alan Chan named identity, delegated permissions across humans, agents and subagents, and provenance of agent-written code, papers, decisions, instructions and cited evidence as key problems with strong demand, and said AI companies need a frictionless solution quickly.
- Dazza Greenwood raised attribution and durable authorization statements (see topic 2).
- Sanjam Garg shared a recent paper on proof of humanhood and said the same tech can be used for proof of "agenthood," with follow-up work in progress.
## 4. Consensus, throughput and security without a token
- Kartik Nayak asked what "does not scale" means (is it validator consensus throughput?), and asked how security works without a token: with Ethereum, security comes from many parties losing ether if something reverts, so "in this world, I guess what does security mean?" He noted his research focus is latency, throughput and resilience, and shared an S&P 2026 paper on deriving latency-throughput bounds under assumptions about parties, delay bound, client rate and bandwidth. His point: protocols should be benchmarked against those bounds rather than claiming they beat everything.
- Mingxun Zhou (HKUST) summarized the design as a tokenless public bulletin needing high efficiency, high throughput and public verifiability, asked how consensus works, and reacted to the stated scale: "That kind of scale, that's like very big, right." He also asked whether OV is for-profit or non-profit.
## 5. Post-quantum proofs, ZK and private retrieval
- Charalampos Papamanthou said the work is "extremely close to what I'm really passionate about" (his PhD was on authenticated data structures, which the recording transcribes as "finicky data structure"), and that he has "at least three projects at Yale" relevant to the post-quantum SNARK direction.
- Mingxun Zhou: his group includes a student heading back to HKUST for a PhD on post-quantum ZK and prospective students on private information retrieval. He is working with Bitcoin Core researchers on lightweight client design using oblivious retrieval, and cares about practical deployment, citing PIR in production. He asked whether a PIR call would be a grand call or one engineering problem, and suggested small challenges such as writing a circuit for a hash function with the fewest gates. He also raised agents discussing with each other without a human signing an NDA as a possible related use case.
## 6. Participation, incentives and grant program design
- Uwe Serdült (Univ. of Zurich, ZDA) said "I don't have the silver bullet," and that OV has to build a community. From his participation research: people do not participate constantly, participation is power-law distributed, and it is selective, driven by intrinsic or extrinsic motivation and tangible feedback. Example: Taiwan's online petitions work because government must give a qualified response. Swiss voters reach about 80% cumulative participation over four years. Internet voting lowers cost, especially for the roughly 10% of Swiss voters abroad. He sees parallels to his work on DAOs and Swiss consultation procedures.
- Sanjam Garg explained Berkeley's models: grants carry about 69% overhead, gifts about 13 to 13.5%, and milestone-contingent funding does not fit a gift and is hard for universities to accept. Alternatives: direct fellowships or funding through independent nonprofits. On size, $10,000 cannot support students or postdocs while about $100,000 (a postdoc for a year) can; many small awards create overhead. He called the proposed middle ground reasonable and asked how many grants and how much funding.
- Charalampos Papamanthou described severe academic funding constraints: a well-reviewed $600,000, three-year NSF proposal went unfunded and took two years to decide. He suggested internships or student programs, a team that identifies pressing problems and issues calls for proposals, possibly funding individuals rather than universities, and interdisciplinary workshops. He compared open problems with prizes to Ethereum Foundation style programs and said he has a spring 2027 sabbatical.
- Dazza Greenwood distinguished direct consulting from grant-funded independent research: "you can't tell Stanford what their conclusions will be." For work that must fit OV's views he favored consulting ("direct consulting sounds like the right shape"), and noted "Stanford's not Accenture," so student clubs and on-campus work sit separately. He proposed three deliverables: draft research questions, a decision matrix of Stanford engagement models, and executing the chosen model. He supports a cross-disciplinary workshop and working group.
- Kartik Nayak advised that university and external rules each apply and that finding their intersection should make arrangements work, with extra constraints around students, particularly international students (visa limits), and a gift structure within university guidelines.
## 7. Legal, liability and compliance
- Prashant Pilla asked about AI trading agents: "if I were to, like, gain losses from that, like, would it be the entity that's responsible or me?" He said he had given models access to accounts without considering it, and suggested "Maybe they should make that a thing, because I feel like people would want to be able to allocate power, but I know it's complicated."
