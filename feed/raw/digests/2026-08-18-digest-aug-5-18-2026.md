# Digest — Aug 5–18, 2026

**TL;DR:** 21 calls. Sharpest, most repeated pushback: trust and incentives — who wants this, who pays, how a tokenless service stays funded. Strong clusters on ordering/scalability and on how rules and proofs get checked.

Period: Aug 5–18, 2026 · Calls covered: 21
Covered 21 recorded external calls (all with pipeline contacts) held Aug 5 to Aug 18. Headline: the sharpest, most repeated pushback was on trust and incentives (who actually wants this, who pays, and how a tokenless service stays funded and stable), alongside a strong cluster of questions on ordering and scalability and on how rules and proofs get checked.
## 1. Ordering, consensus and scalability
- Rüdiger Kapitza (FAU) framed OV as a minimal common base layer for cross-chain interaction and called "an ordering infrastructure for global event scale" an essential building block. His concerns: "Order and persist... It should be scalable." He asked "Is it federated?... Is it really global?" and argued a single global order is "likely to become a bottleneck", suggesting ordering "in a federated or decentralized manner where it matters", possibly hierarchical or DNS-like. He also asked "do you want to lock these events forever?" and whether event lifetime or an expiration date would be decided up front.
- Dennis Hofheinz (ETH Zurich) asked how canonical temporal ordering and consensus would be ensured, noting it is not obviously something you can prove directly. He also raised throughput, data volume, and decentralization/permissionlessness feasibility.
- Jérémie Decouchant (TU Delft) flagged challenges around fair ordering, spam and accountability, and nodes tampering with message order. From his own work: fair ordering forces sequential execution ("with fair ordering, basically you're back to no parallelism"), which is still an open problem at the execution layer. He also asked how this differs from current designs and noted that events added to the log could later prove invalid.
- Scott Shenker (ICSI/Berkeley) related it to shared-log sequencing (a public ordering of events, so applications that interact have an ordering), but distinguished his data-center sequencing work from public infrastructure. He questioned anonymity, explaining that removing it would make scaling logs easier.
- Mohsen Lesani (UCSC) tied it to his work on decentralized, subjective trust (Stellar-style quorums), BFT, cross-chain consensus and transactions, and hierarchical caching and consistency: "Trust is actually subjective rather than objective."
- Ranjit Jhala (UCSD) described two verification layers: verifying the logging substrate guarantees ordering, and verifying that implementations meet the spec, then asking what can be said about things above given the substrate's ordering guarantee.
- Leonid Reyzin (BU) said the work is focused on event ordering and sees "a pretty big gap" between canonical ordering at the base layer and coordinating transactions across legal and economic regimes with mutually untrusting parties: "there's a whole lot in between."
## 2. Formal verification, proofs and rule checking
- Andrew Appel (Princeton) probed whether proofs would rest on signed statements (proof-carrying authentication) and what rules govern combining events from different systems (e.g. Visa and Ethereum). His suggestion: OV may want not one fixed rule set but "a framework in which people can encode rules" with a client-side proof checker "so simple that any fool can see that it's actually doing what it claims." He raised trusted-base, unsound axiom/spec, scalability, proof-size/privacy and standardization concerns, and suggested trying higher-order logic or SMT (CVC6) against LF-style checkers. He noted AI is good at generating proofs and a checker guards against hallucination.
- Aaron Stump (Boston College) described small trustworthy proof checkers as his most relevant work, noted Lean's multiple independent checkers and implementation diversity, and asked why requests must be tied to evidence rather than to evidence stored elsewhere, and whether verification would be a secondary service on the base layer. He said verifying the node software against specifications "is definitely a verification problem" and called the project "very, very challenging, and it's exciting."
- Josh Gancher (Northeastern) (verified Rust, verified parsers, TLS/WireGuard/Signal, MPC verification) said agents using zero-knowledge "without guardrails" is "very likely to be insecure", and offered infrastructure for securely writing and using MPC/ZK protocols, especially with agents.
- Ranjit Jhala (UCSD) said low-level formal verification "has gotten a lot easier" and agreed a good harness helps.
- Mohsen Lesani (UCSC) pointed to compositional verification (verify pieces and move up the stack) and formal guarantees for critical value-transfer software.
- Mohammad Sadoghi (ExpoLab / UC Davis) asked what transactional semantics and consistency models must be revisited for autonomous agents across platforms with different governance, including possibility and impossibility results, and a revisit of ACID-style properties for decentralized agentic settings.
## 3. Post-quantum crypto and proof systems
- Dennis Hofheinz (ETH Zurich): for post-quantum, "nothing gets past lattices" in efficiency and maturity right now, with isogenies as a possibility. He advised leaving room in whatever goes into the base layer because lattice work is moving quickly (including new attacks on SVP/CVP-type assumptions and AI-generated results that could change parameters). He suggested learning from lattice credential systems and short-proof systems, warned about random-oracle composition and nesting, and described himself as a theorist interested in recursive SNARKs and feasibility results rather than implementation.
- Steven Galbraith (Auckland) works on cryptanalysis (including AI-assisted attacks on post-quantum schemes) and said the project is "very far from the things that I work on." His ability to contribute depends on whether OV uses standard tools or novel designs, and he has not worked on SNARKs or proof systems.
- Ari Trachtenberg (BU) raised multi-party computation as a promising area and an overlay-style protocol.
- Leonid Reyzin (BU) called a post-quantum VRF a research project that somebody needs to take on (based on the call summary; wording not checked verbatim).
## 4. Agent identity, legal and liability
- James Grimmelmann (Cornell) (law): asked under what circumstances an automated system acts genuinely on behalf of a person or entity. Under common law you generally cannot unilaterally be made a fiduciary; foundation, stewards and participants should disclaim fiduciary status, and courts have so far rejected "fiduciary for everybody using your system" arguments. Whether software agents can bind principals (contract vs. agency/power of attorney) is "complex and messy" and varies by jurisdiction, and linking a blockchain or registry as the authoritative record for title across jurisdictions: "Nobody knows how to do that." He liked that recording events rather than resolved states has legal advantages. Referrals: Carla Reyes (SMU, "If Rockefeller Was a Coder") and Christopher Ottenay / Andrea Tosato ("Digital Commercial Law"). He offered to review any existing legal white papers or prospectuses.
- Ed Fassio (Purdue) described a governance and compliance problem that is already here: "We are already there", "It will change the geopolitical economic landscape forever", and "It's chaos" at current agent scale. He asked what people and existing organizations need to think about with entirely agentic companies and what stops "a 12 year old" from setting one up ("absolutely nothing"), and called for rules or societal norms. He mentioned lawyers, technologists and end users in conflict in his corporate policy reviews. He also asked whether anything is published and whether there is a document repository.
- Vero Estrada-Galiñanes (EPFL / independent) asked what a neutral root of trust means for identity and intermediary roles ("you are in an intermediary then you become an intermediary and identity"), and what the association plans to do about trust and safety.
- Dennis Hofheinz (ETH Zurich) asked how identities are verified and what semantics they have.
## 5. Trust, transparency and use-case / business model
- Gerhard Schwabe (UZH): his central concern is institutional trust and trust anchoring, not just protocol soundness. Blockchain in logistics struggled on a basic question of who wants transparency; transparency can be a source of power and be abused geopolitically, and in Europe the digital euro is built around one trusted institution rather than trusting a protocol. He advised finding trusted institutions and a concrete use case, who needs inter-organizational trust and who will pay, and raised governance, cartel/antitrust and consortium membership issues. He cautioned that protocols safe today may fail under quantum computing. He cited the Cicada project (car lifecycle as events across companies with selective disclosure) as very similar to OV. On economics: the value of a single record's history may be under a franc to some buyers while insurers buy thousands, so "you have to build markets on those." He also said he has not been active in blockchain research for about five years.
- Leonid Reyzin (BU) had "trouble with the abstract notion of trust", noting that we trust different entities for different purposes (WebPKI mapping, archives, banks, ordered logs), and asked what kind of safety and which trust OV means (timestamping, ordering, identity or money movement).
- Scott Shenker (ICSI/Berkeley): "The Internet ecosystem sucks at crossing borders of authority", and "there's a gaping need" for it; he did not start from financial transactions. Adoption advice from his SDN experience: "get lucky," find an urgent unmet need, enable incremental adoption.
- Rüdiger Kapitza (FAU) and Vero Estrada-Galiñanes (EPFL / independent) both asked what the service model is: Rüdiger on how a tokenless service stays stable and available, Vero on whether this competes with or improves bridges and what actors it targets. Vero said the transparency approach was "excellent".
- Ling Ren (UIUC) said she has stayed away of major crypto projects because many "tunneled into the coin price" and hype; a tokenless, non-coin-price-driven system "is very attractive", and bringing systems together as an anchor is "very interesting to me."
## 6. Reversibility, fraud and safety
- Ari Trachtenberg (BU): banks keep a way to "go back in time if there's fraud discovered", and "the agent can be completely authenticated" yet be steered improperly, so there "needs to be a way to rewind the transactions after the fact." He saw it as fundamentally an exchange between networks without interpolation or execution, and said public event data creates a public telemetry set that can be analyzed. He warned that optimization leaks information and that uniformity is impossible. He called it "a very ambitious plan."
- Leonid Reyzin (BU) warned that efficient money movement does not prevent scams or pyramid schemes and may make them easier; a root of trust "may get you on a faster train to hell" (from call summary; wording not checked verbatim). He accepted it as necessary but not sufficient.
## 7. Incentives and economics
- Nigel Smart (independent, consultant to Zama and others): academics respond to work already on their critical path or to a large incentive: "there's nothing in between." Zama funds academic work by phrasing a problem three or four years out in the academic's own terminology. He noted that commercial cryptography companies are now product and customer-focused ("everyone's focusing on real customers and real end cases") and he knows no academics who would map OV's proposal into their work.
- Mohsen Lesani (UCSC) asked whether there would be a token ("No token"), and agreed another incentive mechanism is needed, noting Bitcoin's mining reward is what keeps miners behaving.
- Rüdiger Kapitza (FAU): bootstrapping without money is fine initially, but eventually participants need an incentive to run it, as DNS providers have.
- Leonid Reyzin (BU) asked how OV is funded in the long term and noted that giving away money does not last, and that participation could become prestigious (brand equity at the root of trust).
## 8. Adoption, program design and research engagement
- Timing and incentive structure. Nigel Smart: "PhD students work in years", and a fall-semester sprint is "useless for a PhD student". Ling Ren: fundamental problems may not progress in six months or a year (some of hers have run six or seven years), so timelines may not align. Mohammad Sadoghi: formal awards can take a year or two, so use small university seed gifts ($25k to $50k) in the meantime. Josh Gancher: typical engagements run 12 to 24 months. Leonid Reyzin: raised university scheduling, compensation, administrative constraints and buyouts.
- Trust in the process. Ari Trachtenberg: faculty need to know how proposals are judged and how objective the process is; many corporate RFPs are staged or underfunded and "faculty are fairly jaded." Vero: grant contests are often opaque and grants may be too small for senior researchers; she asked for a transparent selection process and grant sizes. Josh Gancher asked for a document on how the initiative works and whether there will be a call for proposals.

## Open questions for R&D
1. Is the event order global, federated or hierarchical, and how is storage organized and scaled? (Kapitza, Hofheinz)
1. Are events locked forever, or is there a lifetime or expiration? (Kapitza)
1. What exactly is formally proven in the state machine model, and how are identities verified and given semantics? (Hofheinz)
1. Should the rule/proof checker be a general framework in which clients encode their own rules, and what logic or checker (LF, SMT/CVC6, HOL) fits? (Appel, Stump)
1. What are the trusted base and soundness risks of the specification and checker? (Appel)
1. Does fair ordering force sequential execution, and what is the fairness and performance tradeoff? (Decouchant)
1. How do you handle spam, accountability and message-order tampering at the base layer? (Decouchant)
1. What should be left open in the base layer given fast-moving lattice and post-quantum research? (Hofheinz)
1. Which kind of trust does OV provide (timestamping, ordering, identity or money movement), and what does "safety" mean? (Reyzin)
1. How are fraud and agent misbehavior handled without rewind or chargeback? (Trachtenberg, Reyzin)
1. What transactional semantics and consistency properties hold for autonomous agents across platforms, and what are the possibility and impossibility results? (Sadoghi)
1. How does a tokenless service stay funded, stable and available long term? (Kapitza, Reyzin, Lesani)
1. Is the system standard tools or novel designs? (Galbraith, Decouchant)
1. How should software agents be given authority to bind principals, and could stewards or participants be fiduciaries? (Grimmelmann)
1. What is the concrete use case, who pays, and who wants this transparency? (Schwabe, Smart)
