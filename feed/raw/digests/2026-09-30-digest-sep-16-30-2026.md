# Digest — Sep 16–30, 2026

**TL;DR:** 17 calls. Tokenless incentives raised independently by at least 4 people; skepticism about total ordering across 3 calls; large agent identity, reputation and sybil cluster.

Period: Sep 16 – Sep 30, 2026 · Calls covered: 17 (16 professors/researchers, 2 industry)
17 external calls spanning NYU, TU/e, Ruhr University Bochum, USI, UPenn, UZH, Yale, Microsoft Research, Google, Adobe, Georgia Tech, Michigan, RWTH Aachen, Georgetown/Bar-Ilan (Ligero), and Illinois. Biggest recurring theme: incentive design without tokens raised independently by at least four people (Pratyush Mishra, Daniel Genkin, Fernando Pedone, Shabsi Walfish); skepticism about total ordering as the right abstraction across three calls (Ghassan Karame, Dominique Unruh, Fernando Pedone).
## Consensus, Ordering & Scaling
- Ghassan Karame (Ruhr University Bochum) questioned whether total ordering is necessary at all, suggesting eventual consistency and trusted timestamp servers might suffice; referenced Hyperledger Fabric's separation of ordering from execution. Called the OV thesis "very ambitious" and "a very nice scientific problem to solve" but skeptical on scalability: "Nothing of the sort exists today." Presented forthcoming work on GossipSub, Turbine (Solana), and Bitcoin's random graph topology showing critical robustness failures.
- Fernando Pedone (USI) raised the scaling challenge of ordering everything through a central layer: "everything goes through this layer" and much of it "doesn't care to be ordered." From Cosmos/ICF experience: Tendermint's engine was "coded together with the ordering part and it was very hard to separate them." Observed "Cosmos would be closer to your vision than Ethereum somehow."
- Anirudh Sivaraman (NYU) connected OV's ordering layer to his work on programmable scheduling and synchronized timestamps: "timestamps are inherently sort of random variables and you should be treating them as such." Referenced social choice theory approaches (Pompeii, Cornell) and front-running issues.
- Pratyush Mishra (UPenn) suggested scaling consensus, verifying consensus, and scaling data availability checks as "immediate" research directions aligned with his SNARK expertise.
- Atul Prakash (Michigan) raised state synchronization and concurrency control concerns in distributed logs (Google Docs analogy); suggested batching and block creation as practical ordering approaches.
- Josh Benaloh (Microsoft Research): "it feels to me like a blockchain is a very poor way to ensure accurate ordering because miners can change the order of transactions." Acknowledged OV's ledger-of-actions approach differs from traditional blockchains.
## Incentive & Economic Design (Tokenless Model)
- Pratyush Mishra (UPenn): "How do you incentivize the participants to actually maintain this network? Because it requires a lot of resources, I presume if you're doing an Internet scale especially."
- Daniel Genkin (Georgia Tech): "What is my incentive to participate?" Without tokens, "you end up with a trusted party which is antithetical to the whole premise. Or 17 trusted parties in this case." Asked for operational clarity: "What are you guys trying to achieve? The problem compiles. But like operationally, what's the goals here?"
- Fernando Pedone (USI): "I have a hard time understanding your business model" and "there is the issue of how do you bootstrap this." Characterized early-stage investment as "an investment kind of without any real clear path."
- Shabsi Walfish (Google): the system is "not just a protocol because it's stateful and therefore will require resource investments from some set of participants to sustain it." Drew analogy to Internet networking protocols where "people find various economic incentives to provide services or connectivity."
## Post-Quantum Crypto & Proof Systems
- Pratyush Mishra (UPenn) described his pivot toward post-quantum SNARKs, matching pre-quantum efficiency in proof sizes and prover time; different applications may have different proof size requirements (5KB vs 50KB). Called the fellowship program "fantastic, especially in a world where federal funding is getting decimated."
- Ben Fisch (Yale): "Are you independently interested in pushing the boundary of proof size for post-quantum proofs, or do you think of that more as a subcomponent?" Asked whether OV has "already identified an application that relies on more performant post-quantum proofs." Andreas: "one of many really important questions."
- Dominique Unruh (RWTH Aachen) expressed strong interest in time-stamped proofs in a ledger enabling non-PQ-secure proofs to remain reliable retroactively; proposed a transition approach from classical to PQ security without losing old data. Frustrated with published quantum proofs being "kind of a bit fishy," leading him to formal verification of quantum cryptography.
- Lorenzo Grassi (TU/e) noted his symmetric crypto work (Poseidon, integer rings) may not exactly fit OV's needs, but TU/e colleagues working on PQ protocols "could be really interested"; agreed to forward materials.
- Katerina Sotiraki (Yale) began asking about PQ cryptography directions before the call ended.
## Agent Identity, Reputation & Sybil Resistance
- Shabsi Walfish (Google) raised the most detailed identity concerns: revocation of stolen identities, reputation fragmentation ("how do you prevent fragmented ecosystem of reputation where I can repeatedly defraud people with the same identity from different vantage points"), unlimited spam identity creation. Proposed reputation attaching to data-center ownership and compute resources — hardware-based accountability.
- Ghassan Karame (Ruhr University Bochum) raised the "Mafia attack" concern: probabilistic AI agents cannot be assumed deterministic — "you cannot assume deterministic behavior" with modern AI models.
- Atul Prakash (Michigan) raised collusion risks: agents could "collude and create a good transaction history which makes us look good and honest" before defecting. Noted "different agents could look at the same history and come to different answers." Suggested game-theoretic Sybil prevention via auction mechanisms; raised privacy concerns about transaction exposure.
- Josh Benaloh (Microsoft Research): "Is anonymity a core property for what you're thinking or do you really want hard identities?" Noted the Yelp-reviews problem: "how do we know these are real people or real entities."
- Leonard Rosenthol (Adobe/C2PA): "this is where authorization and delegation come in" — defining what agents are authorized to do and on whose behalf, drawing on C2PA content-provenance experience.
- Vishal Sachdev (Illinois) saw OV enabling "scalable proof of ID for machine or piece of software or agents"; cautioned against excessive technology that people won't adopt.
## Trust Infrastructure & Legal/Liability Frameworks
- Daniel Genkin (Georgia Tech): "you need a piece of trusted hardware that can execute all this where the agents cannot escape." Saw TEEs as relevant but questioned the incentive model without tokens.
- Dominique Unruh (RWTH Aachen) had difficulty connecting ordering to legal liability: "I don't understand what it has to do precisely with ordering. Why not just having a signature?" Identified a "chicken-egg problem"; OV requirements are "vague" to him.
- Shabsi Walfish (Google) raised log-spam scalability and legal/regulatory issues around content hosting and redaction ("someone encoded an illegal image into a bunch of DNS names"); questioned the "killer use case" for a global log.
- Esha Ghosh (Microsoft Research) emphasized need for interdisciplinary collaboration (distributed systems, cryptography, identity); questioned whether a fully open distributed protocol is necessary given computational constraints; interested in formal verification of cryptographic protocols; skeptical about achieving true decentralization in practice.
## Cryptography & Formal Verification
- Esha Ghosh (Microsoft Research) presented key transparency for E2E encryption (deployed by Meta, ProtonMail), interoperable encryption (EU DMA mandate), and attacks on MLS-based E2E video calling (4–5 platforms, disclosure ongoing): "MLS was not designed for calling." Found "generic classes of attacks" even on well-analyzed standardized protocols.
- Josh Benaloh (Microsoft Research) discussed ZK proof size improvements for voting (20x reduction from 1MB); noted multilinear approaches were broken but expects solutions. Cautioned "blockchains have been applied way beyond where they should be applied."
- Dominique Unruh (RWTH Aachen) suggested standardized signature schemes when problem-specific requirements are unknown; detailed primitive questions are "impossible to answer without understanding the big protocol first."
## Adoption, Program Design & Fellowship Feedback
- Claudio Tessone (UZH) systematized the thesis effectively: "blockchains solved who owns what while OV addresses what happened." Affirmed need for "environments that allow the emergence of trust between different parties to be executed in a very scalable manner." Suggested Swiss universities have coordination advantages.
- Fernando Pedone (USI): "I can tell you I'm interested in learning more how this is going to work." Found the vision "interesting" and "promising"; agreed to help identify interested colleagues.
- Muthu Venkitasubramaniam (Georgetown/Ligero) raised university bureaucracy timelines: "probably not your first cohort because it takes a long time." Agreed to take materials to his department chair. Carmit Hazay (Bar-Ilan/Ligero) is on sabbatical; both expressed interest in participating via Ligero.
- Ghassan Karame (Ruhr University Bochum) compared OV's program favorably to Polkadot's fellowship, noting greater flexibility; committed to discussing with colleagues Clara and Pedro and disseminating within the Ruhr/Bochum ecosystem.
- Leonard Rosenthol (Adobe) agreed to reach out to C2PA and university contacts; Andreas floated Adobe as a potential future node operator (analogous to ICANN root DNS servers run by Google, AWS, Cisco).
- Josh Benaloh (Microsoft Research) expressed cautious interest in judging selection panels but is "trying to do less work rather than more"; raised concern about "dispersing more money than appropriate without knowing what proposals look like"; affirmed the iterative pre-proposal approach.
- Daniel Genkin (Georgia Tech) willing to submit a proposal on TEE/hardware security topics; cautious about introductions given low yield from typical foundation calls; agreed to review materials first.
