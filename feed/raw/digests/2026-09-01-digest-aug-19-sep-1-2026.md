# Digest — Aug 19–Sep 1, 2026

**TL;DR:** 21 calls. Several people independently doubted a tokenless, decentralized ordering layer can reach Internet scale and questioned participation incentives; proof size is the other hard constraint.

Period: Aug 19–Sep 1, 2026 · Calls covered: 21
This covers 21 external calls with pipeline contacts held Aug 19 to Sep 1 (assigned by recording date, UTC). 20 had a usable transcript and one (Rolf Weber, Aug 28) never connected. The biggest recurring theme: several people independently doubted that a tokenless, decentralized ordering layer can reach Internet scale, and questioned who has the incentive to take part. Proof size is the other hard constraint. Coverage note: the transcripts are long, so what follows reflects the portions extractable from each call. Later parts of some calls may hold additional points.
## 1. Ordering and consensus: feasibility and scope
- Gil Segev (HUJI, on leave leading crypto research at Coinbase) said the thesis made sense but "you need some sort of, you know, blockchain that operates 10x or maybe 100x compared to any existing blockchain, and then you need some additional features." He advised OV not to position itself around goals "not known to be achievable" from a research perspective. As a researcher he said it sounds like "another Monday," but other people may balk.
- Alysson Bessani (Univ. of Lisbon, consensus/BFT since 2001) said he is "very skeptical" of new consensus protocols: "PBFT is better than this unless you really know you need a lot of machines." On scale: "I don't believe it's possible to have just a single ordering for the whole Internet." Ordering may work for domains where it makes sense. He suggested many deployments or rollups sharing one ordering service with different groups. He asked whether OV needs a total order even across independent events (Andreas Freund called this open: one absolute order or order within a domain). He also said million-events-per-second results usually come from controlled, best-case benchmarks.
- Alexey Gotsman (IMDEA) reframed OV as a decentralized ordering service and asked what distinguishes it technically beyond participant count and transaction volume. He also asked whether the token-free design points toward Stellar-style federated or named-trust algorithms (like the 17 DNS root nodes). The OV answer was that no monolithic consensus algorithm is expected and the design is likely layered. Manuel Bravo (Informal Systems) asked who maintains the network of trust.
- Mario Larangeira (Tokyo/IOG, Hydra) said tokenless "might be a block." Ordering may be less of a problem for UTXO-based Hydra than for Ethereum. He stressed that liveness is needed alongside ordering and asked whether storage is a separate entity. His group has released proof-of-replication work and keeps consensus and data-availability roles separate ("We don't mix them").
- Omer Reingold (Stanford) asked what information determines a neutral order: "if it's just based on arrival order, then there is nothing to work on." He later said he could see the importance but still did not see what fairness questions arise, perhaps because it is early. He recommended the call for proposals include a technical description so researchers outside the usual area can see how to relate.
- Marten van Dijk (CWI) noted that a bulletin-board model adds a party that could itself be a weak point, and asked whether OV wants decentralized solutions.
- Magnus Myreen (Chalmers) asked where events are stored so both parties can query them (OV: not all events need ordering; storage and availability are open).
## 2. Post-quantum cryptography, proof systems and proof size
- Christopher Peikert (UMich) said post-quantum is essential and the architecture needs fairly functional cryptography (vector and functional commitments) to scale. He agreed signature size (e.g. Falcon) is not the main bottleneck; data availability and sampling are. He called the target of proofs under about 5 KB on consumer hardware a "moon shot" and doubted current techniques can get under 100 KB without a major paradigm change.
- Eylon Yogev (Bar-Ilan, ZK/SNARKs, works with the Ethereum Foundation on post-quantum Ethereum) said proof size is crucial for throughput. Recursive aggregation keeps proof size constant. He cited his hash-based SNARK lower-bound work and said getting below a kilobyte is likely unrealistic. He is interested in the open problems of heterogeneous state machines and recursive proofs, and shared his hash-based proofs book.
- Stefano Tessaro (UW) asked whether OV already has a cryptographic wish list or architecture, or is just assuming a fast enough SNARK. He said that of a small-proof goal, "If we could do that, that would be amazing."
- Mario Larangeira named proof sizes and setup ceremonies as ZK bottlenecks. Lior Rotem (HUJI) said post-quantum security is needed regardless of OV, but the combination of decentralized, scalable and tokenless is what makes it hard. He works on post-quantum ZK proofs and post-quantum secret single-leader election.
- Dan Bernstein (UIC) prefers basic, application-agnostic primitives. He is most drawn to signatures and their failure modes ("what can we do proactively to stop anything from going wrong?"). He spends little time on SNARKs.
- Vincent Rijmen (KU Leuven, AES co-designer) offered help on fundamental proofs and hash-function properties and guarantees. Hardware and side-channel work is his main area, and fintech is not his specialty.
- Christian Majenz (DTU) said cryptographic outputs should have broader uses than a time-ordering proof layer. He mentioned ongoing work on new SNARK architectures.
## 3. Tokenless design and incentives
- Asked independently by Chenglu Jin (CWI) ("if there is no token, how do you plan to incentivize people to join this permissionless network?"), Alysson Bessani (how people are incentivized and how identity or permissioning works without tokens; he called both still open), Christian Majenz (how consensus works without token economics), Alexey Gotsman and Mario Larangeira. Jack Jia answered that OV does not have the answer and may revert to a token model if the open R&D shows it is not possible. Lior Rotem said the same.
- Andreas Park (Rotman) said any contributors' incentives need to be assessed. As academics, "our incentive is to publish and ... satisfy our own curiosity," not necessarily the bigger picture.
- Dylan Unruh (Dartmouth, College.xyz) said students need a clear role, time burden and gain.
## 4. Agent trust, spoofing and formal verification
- Magnus Myreen asked how you know agents do not spoof or simulate the service.
- Gil Segev asked whether the identity problem is specifically binding an agent to an ID or something wider. He said crypto systems need math backing, formal specs and verification from theory to spec to code, and that OV-style library composition lacks that.
- Marten van Dijk (CWI) raised setup and trust assumptions, hardware correctness and the lack of confidential digital state. His group uses PUF-based one-time signatures for remote attestation. He said the post-quantum scheme is unpublished and the practical protocol assembly remains.
- Clara Schneidewind (MPI-SP) works on formal verification of cryptographic protocols. Pedro Moreno-Sanchez (IMDEA) works on privacy-preserving payment channels and adapter signatures. Pedro said the minimal-unit service concept connected to his earlier thinking on the Internet of Value.
## 5. Adoption, use cases and market
- Andreas Park asked for a one-sentence end goal and urged "think small": a first use case, a first buyer, and whether firms must coordinate. He noted blockchain buy-in from financial institutions has been "extremely low" and suggested universities as a cheap early adopter. He also warned against a theoretically perfect product with no market (the "German engineering perspective") and said real-world testing is needed.
- Dylan Unruh recommended a tightly scoped student activation with a defined task and fixed funding, modeled on Solana's week-long campus activations. He suggested starting with a research activation workshop, applications over open flexibility, locking in professors first and activating students a month or two later. He said College.xyz can help most once the vision is solidified.
- Gerhard Schwabe (UZH) said people must understand OV's vision to know how to contribute. The proposal process should reduce grant-writing effort (he suggested 3 to 5 pages with a work plan and milestones). He found the website minimalistic.
## 6. Program and proposal design (open call, funding, events)
- Ling Ren (UIUC) said the audience for the proposal event is unclear: faculty PIs are busy, while students and postdocs would rather have a research seminar. She proposed a two-step process (a 1 to 2 page pre-proposal, then a mandatory proposal curation workshop), because "if it's not required I as a PI I would skip it." She questioned per-university events when only 1 to 2 teams per university may be funded, and noted a "winter of blockchain." She suggested a Sep 30 first-round deadline and offered a list of relevant faculty.
- Stefano Tessaro (UW) suggested an Ethereum-style wishlist (a focused subset of priority problems) rather than a fully open call. He raised university concerns about accepting blockchain-origin money and asked how OV is funded.
- Charalampos (Babis) Papamanthou (Yale) described a prior $5M-over-5-years Algorand center that was cut after one year when the token fell. He said funding must go through the university, as a grant with deliverables or a gift, and he flagged tax and jurisdiction questions. He suggested roughly $1M a year for five years plus a possible endowed postdoc, and said "I think this is the right approach."
- Christian Majenz and Clara Schneidewind asked about overhead, postdoc and PhD funding, project length and what nodes contribute. Dan Bernstein asked who the fellows would be and wanted confidentiality markings on shared material. Magnus Myreen asked about joint proposals with a collaborator in Singapore (no participant limit, per Andreas Freund).
- Vincent Rijmen noted that "universities can be slower" and that professors will not take problems that must be solved within a month. He offered to send a menu of cooperation options.

## Open questions for R&D
1. Can a blockchain-class ordering layer run 10x to 100x faster than today's chains, and which parts are research-feasible? (Segev)
1. Do we need one total order for all events, or per-domain ordering with shared infrastructure? (Bessani, Freund)
1. Can the system be tokenless? How do participants get incentives, identity and permissioning without tokens? (Jin, Bessani, Majenz, Gotsman, Larangeira, Rotem)
1. Which consensus family fits: federated or named-trust, layered, or something else? (Gotsman)
1. What information makes an order "neutral", beyond arrival order? What fairness questions arise? (Reingold)
1. What proof size is realistic: under 5 KB, under 100 KB, or not below about a kilobyte? Do we have a cryptographic wish list, or just assume a fast SNARK? (Peikert, Yogev, Tessaro)
1. How are data availability, archiving and event storage handled, and how is liveness assured alongside ordering? (Myreen, Larangeira, Peikert)
1. How do we stop agents from spoofing or simulating the service? (Myreen)
1. How do we handle setup and trust assumptions and hardware correctness for attestation? (van Dijk)
1. What first narrow use case and buyer would prove usefulness without a large network effect? (Park)
1. Should the open call be a focused wishlist or fully open, and what should the proposal and workshop process look like? (Tessaro, Ren, Schwabe)
