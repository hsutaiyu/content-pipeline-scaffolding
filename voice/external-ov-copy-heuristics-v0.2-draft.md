# OV copy heuristics

Draft v0.2, 29 September 2026. Rules for auditing and drafting OV social copy: LinkedIn, X, YouTube captions and Shorts, Substack Notes. Essays and personal-handle posts are out of scope and are never ghostwritten.

---

## 0. How to use this file

Run every draft through the sections in order: accuracy (1), slop (2), register (3), voice (4), structure (5), closers (6), format (7). A draft that fails section 1 is rejected no matter how it reads.

Score each post on three axes:

| Axis | Pass | Minor | Fail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thesis accuracy | Accurate | Minor fix | Inaccurate |
| AI slop | Few tells | Some tells | Heavy tells |
| Voice | Good start | Partial | Off |

These heuristics govern register, terminology, doctrine, and structural discipline. They do not govern argument structure: structural invention within section 5 is welcome. Review a new shape against the rules, never against the shape of previous pieces.

## 1. Accuracy

### 1.1 The spine

Every post expands one of these three sentences. Name which one before drafting. If a post expands none of them, question whether it should exist.

1. Value is not native to the Internet, so we rely on trust intermediaries over the peer-to-peer Internet of information to move it.
2. Blockchains did something genuinely well here, coupling computation and coordination so that ownership and order could be agreed on.
3. What happened across heterogeneous economic systems, with many roots of trust, still remains elusive at Internet level.

Once the research program is public (1.6), posts may also expand one of the two research questions.

### 1.2 What OV is

- An **open research initiative**. Never an "open standards initiative", a product, a platform, a network, or a project launch.
- Identity: **a draft of an idea**, and researchers asking researchers for help.
- What it researches: a neutral root of trust that records **what happened and in what order, and never asks what it means**. This is the site's wording; that sentence shape is allowed only there.
- It is additive. It sits alongside existing Internet services and serves trust intermediaries, which are the customers. It never replaces, routes around, or disintermediates them.
- Name: "OV" or "Open Value". Never "IOV", "IOVI", "Internet of Open Value" or "Internet of Value". Settled 2026-10-05; avoid hard-coding the name in banked assets where possible.

### 1.3 What to call it

- When saying what OV **is**: **a new Internet service** or **an ordering service**. Never "a layer" as its category, and "missing layer" is banned everywhere.
- When a piece works through the **mechanism**, the established terms are the working vocabulary: **base layer**, **shared layer**, **neutral, shared substrate**, **neutral coordination layer**. The fuller coordination form leads when the opener introduces it ("A neutral coordination layer that orders events without assigning meaning…"). On video: **a neutral coordination substrate**. Pick one per piece; do not rotate, and never coin new ones.
- "Base layer" is always correct inside verbatim research-question quotes.
- The artifact is **the shared record**. Systems participate by **connecting to the shared record**; prefer the participation verb over positional ones ("systems connecting to it", not "systems above it"). For what sits above, use a concrete subject: "Systems and domains above it retain their own semantics, execution, and rules."
- At the sentence carrying the piece's main move, prefer the named subject ("OV", "the base layer") over a generic reference ("the research", "it").

### 1.4 What OV is not

Treat each item as a hard fail.

- **Not a settlement layer, payment rail, or interoperability protocol.** Settlement, payments, execution, contracts, and identity all belong to the semantic layer, above OV.
- **It interprets nothing.** It records and orders events. It never interprets, executes, or validates them, and it runs no programs.
- **Not a blockchain and not in anyone's ecosystem.** Not EVM-native, not an Ethereum project, not the world computer, not inside any intermediary's ecosystem.
- **Not a finished design.** It is a hypothesis. Nothing downstream of separating event from state is settled.
- **The token question is open.** "OV has no token" is fine as a present fact. "Tokenless by design", "never a token", and any promise that tokenless will work are all banned. Never mention token plans, mainnets, or L1s.
- **No working groups.** There are none.
- **No money figures.** No grant totals, pool sizes, or any other number about funding.
- **No unconfirmed program facts.** No dates, deadlines, IP terms, licences, or process details until confirmed in writing.
- **Do not announce pre-proposals.** They are private, informal, and sent by email.

### 1.5 Definitions

Use these words consistently, one meaning each.

| Term | Use |
|---|---|
| Event | The fact that something happened. |
| State | What a system concludes once it interprets events, such as a balance, a contract in force, or an owner of record. |
| Order | The position an event holds in a sequence. Local inside one system, contested across systems. |
| Value | Define it narrowly, in examples, **only when the post makes a claim about value**. Never as a token, smart contracts, or program state. |
| Semantic layer | Any system above the service (a bank, a registry, a platform) that reads the shared record and derives its own state. |

Value has no single canonical wording; do not paste a definition into every post. Sanctioned forms: positive assignment with the term marked ("The research uses the term 'value' to refer to the records of economically consequential events."), negative assignment ("Value here does not refer to the asset itself, or the application state in an arbitrary system."), and a plain gloss paired with the term ("what changed, who was involved, and the context around it").

### 1.6 The research program (once public)

- Program content begins only after the public announcement. Source it only from the live site: the two **Key Research Questions** and **How to participate**.
  - **3.1 Neutrality without a token:** can the service operate neutrally (no censorship) and safely (strong security and data availability guarantees) at Internet scale without a token?
  - **3.2 Native exchange between semantic layers:** what must be true for different, untrusted semantic layers to exchange value natively through the service?
- Open to builders, researchers, standards bodies, and enterprises. Everything published is open, citable, and credited.
- Never say "six open problems" or give a numbered list beyond the two questions.
- Frame selection as peer to peer, never as a review board or selection committee.

### 1.7 Tone rules that are really accuracy rules

- **Credit before contrast.** Say what blockchains did well before anything else, and say intermediaries "do real work". Never "tried and fell short" or "got close".
- **No villains.** Never talk badly about intermediaries or institutions. Avoid scenes built to make them look slow or bad.
- **No fear, no FOMO, no urgency built on threat.** No chaos, collapse, "trust is breaking", runaway agents, or geopolitics.
- **Why now, stated neutrally:** value moving between parties online keeps rising, while trust in any single party to hold the record isn't keeping pace. Those two lines should move together.
- **Don't lead with agents.** Talk about scaling.
- **Research tense.** "OV is researching whether", never "OV does" or "OV will".

## 2. Slop: the tells that give machine copy away

Tells are about **sentence shape**, not vocabulary. Check every item.

### 2.1 Structural tells

1. **Rule-of-three fragment stack.** "Verifiable. Unowned. Open." Write one sentence that says the thing.
2. **"It's not X. It's Y." reversal.** Say what it is.
3. **Symmetrical antithesis.** "records what happened, and never asks what it means" is allowed only as the site's own wording. Elsewhere, vary the clause lengths.
4. **Choppy one-line paragraphs for rhythm.** Write a long explanatory sentence, then one short, flat one.
5. **Even cadence**, every sentence 12 to 18 words. Mix lengths: a 40-word explanation next to a 5-word verdict.
6. **Rhetorical opener with no answer** ("What if…", "Imagine…", "So what is it?"). Ask the question and answer it in the next sentence, or open on a fact or a scene. (A hypothesis-first "What if" that names the subject and its terms immediately is an allowed opener; see 3.2 R5.)
7. **Setup openers** that withhold the subject for staging ("It is worth being exact about…"). Definition-first naming is allowed ("Two concepts are central to this research: events and state."); what is banned is withholding the subject for effect. Start on the fact.
8. **Tidy closing summary** ("The question is open. The work is beginning."). Stop when the point is made, or end on the open question itself.
9. **Abstract nouns doing the work** ("infrastructure for the agentic economy"). Name the concrete thing.
10. **Significance landings** ("this changes everything", "paradigm shift", "the future of"). The short line states a fact or a boundary, never significance.
11. **Sayings that sound deep** — aphorisms that invert or parallel for profundity. Say the literal thing.
12. **Shallow -ing riders** ("…, highlighting its importance"). Cut the rider; keep the fact.
13. **Device metaphors that explain themselves.** A structure may carry a piece; a figure may not. Metaphor is drafting scaffolding: flatten it at settlement. The sequence is the device, not the figure, and never the visible signpost labels.

### 2.2 Salesmanship tells

Anything that reads as marketing is out, even when the underlying claim is accurate.

- **Teaser clauses** that withhold the subject for effect ("a cost that is easy to miss").
- **Setup-and-reveal scaffolding** ("The candidate answer:"). State the scenario impersonally and fold the answer into a conditional.
- **Invitation framings of settled doctrine** ("published so anyone can argue with them"). State the fact flat: "The questions and the specifications are published openly."
- **Meta-commentary about the piece's own hedge or structure.** A scope hedge reports the work done ("Our review did not identify a system that has been built along these lines."), never discusses itself.
- **Mid-piece series framing** ("the question this series is really about") reads as inflation. Series pointers are optional and live at the close.

### 2.3 Repetition across the calendar, and canonical phrases

Repetition across posts is the biggest cross-post tell.

- **Never reuse a sentence** from another post in the previous four weeks. This includes the value definition.
- **Canonical phrases: fixed wording, managed frequency.** The following are settled language. When one appears, its wording is verbatim; never paraphrase them. But each is also a beat, and overuse turns doctrine into wallpaper — state once per continuity window (5.2), then compress to a mechanism-only reference or cut. Do not force one into a piece whose argument doesn't need it.
  - "State is always derived from events." (the anchor; see R15)
  - "without relying on a designated arbiter" (text posts)
  - "execution rules, economic incentives, and governance"
  - "remains an open question" / "remains to be explored" / "left open for examination" (the explicit labels; see R6)
  - "That is (OV's) working hypothesis." (the standalone label; see R15)
- **One noun for OV per post** (from the sanctioned set in 1.3). Never rotate or coin synonyms — elegant variation is a tell.
- **One idea a day, written once per platform.** Never post the same argument on LinkedIn and again on X a few days apart.

### 2.4 Word level

- **Banned outright:** thrilled, seamless, delve, unlock, empower, revolutionary, excited, game-changing, disrupt, interoperability, composability, trustless, web3, leverage, crucial, pivotal, testament, underscore, showcase, boast, renowned, "commitment to", "deep dive", "at the end of the day", "moving forward", "missing layer", "bridging the gap", "the last closed layer", "the TCP/IP of X" (TCP/IP is only allowed to explain layering), "the real question is", "at its core", "what really matters", "fundamentally", flawed, "doesn't work", "must rely on".
- **Filler, always cut:** "essentially", "Note that", "therefore", "in order to", "due to the fact that", "it is important to note that".
- **Qualified:** "however" is banned as a clause-initial tic but required mid-clause when a contrast needs an explicit connective ("Across systems, however, …"). "simply" is allowed as a softener inside definitions ("An event is simply the fact that something happened") and cut elsewhere; minimizing phrasing ("nothing more than") is always out.
- **Vague connectors** ("associated with", "linked to", "tied to") are out unless the relationship is named.
- **"Worth ___" openers:** banned.

### 2.5 Mechanics

- No em dashes and no double hyphens in published copy. Use a comma, colon, or full stop.
- **"Internet" is always capitalised**, including "Internet scale" and "Internet level".
- Curly quotes and apostrophes.
- Dates are month-first: "December 31", not "31 December".
- Trailing prepositional phrases take no comma: "a designated arbiter inside one network".
- Formal modal: "must", not "has to".
- Don't strand a short word at a line end; don't leave one word alone on the last line.
- No hashtags, no emoji bullets, no markdown headers or decorative bold, no literal `<br>`. Term marking is the exception, by platform convention: italics on X threads and render cards (first use; every occurrence when the word itself is the piece's subject; the load-bearing emphasis word in card copy), quotation marks on LinkedIn and video. Italics also mark spoken-delivery emphasis in video scripts.
- A "Learn more" link may precede the standing closer. Every link is real at publish time; no placeholder URLs in published copy.

## 3. Register and word choice

### 3.1 Platform tiers

**Text posts (X, LinkedIn):** the full register stack (R1–R16) applies at full strength.

**Video, shorts, reels (scripts, descriptions, captions):** conversational register. What changes:

- **Hooks are required.** Negate the obvious misconception in plain speech, then ask the direct question ("Value isn't the asset itself. So what is it?"). A declarative contrast line may also lead, question following ("Nobody has ever been paid to settle which copy of an email is real. So why can information be copied, and value cannot?").
- Plain-language glosses replace technical nouns in supporting lists. The gloss is not an incantation — its components may reorder and extend; the canonical term it accompanies stays fixed.
- Conversational mechanics are in: sentence-starting "But" and "So", contractions, short one-beat paragraphs.
- Plain outcome language and clear idioms stay ("The Internet made information move.", "elusive"). Design-intent framing (R4) and idiom-to-mechanism precision (R7) still apply at text strength.
- The arbiter formulation may be glossed plainly ("with nothing but code"); the formal form is text-only.
- Verbs: "computed" for what a consensus network does to its record ("the network that computed it"); a generic record-keeper "notes" or "records" — do not overclaim a mechanism the piece has not specified.
- Mark examples as examples: "Take one event for instance…"
- The closer may ask the question the full piece answers. The standing closer may be dropped when the piece closes on a forward open question; the question form is its own classification.

**What does not change on video:** canonical core terms verbatim, additive framing, classification honesty, no hype, no praise of the technology (R2), no defensive lines (R3).

### 3.2 Register rules

- **R1. Institutional-formal register.** No deliberate fragments, no habitual sentence-starting "And"/"But" (one deliberate "But" pivot per piece is allowed), no deadpan aphoristic endings, no negation triads ("does not refer to…", not "not a token, not smart contracts, not program state").
- **R2. Neutral description, never evaluation.** State what the technology introduced and let the fact stand; no praise of the achievement. Evaluating the *problem* is permitted ("Blockchains addressed an important problem on the Internet."). The blockchain achievement verb is "demonstrated", never "established" — it reports a result shown inside one network, not a settled fact founded.
- **R3. No defensive or pre-emptive lines.** State the position without defending it against an imagined objection. Cut "That is not a flaw." and its family.
- **R4. Design intent, not outcome (text posts).** "Was built to X" over "made X happen"; "cannot" over "never did". Video keeps plain outcome language.
- **R5. Impersonal framing by default; first person must earn its place.** The collective "we" earns it inside the research question itself ("can we preserve agreement on the ordering of events while leaving their meaning open…"), in the act of posing it ("Instead of asking what a system must accept, we ask what a record would have to leave out."), and in series framing. Second person is unaffected: direct address is fine ("If you have dealt with conflicting databases or ledgers, you have seen…").
- **R6. Open problems as process, labeled explicitly.** "Still taking shape" over "not settled"; "No comparable network has been deployed yet" over "none has shipped". Use the explicit label mid-piece ("remains an open question") and the invitational form at the close ("remains to be explored", "left open for examination"). The failure-mode confession has two lengths: the long form spells the failure out ("Stripping meaning out may simply move the problem up a layer…"); the short form is acknowledgment plus the work ("We do not know, and testing the hypothesis is the work.").
- **R7. Name the mechanism; carry the causal chain to the actual reason.** Prefer the technically exact phrase over the accessible gloss. Where an idiom names a failure or limit, replace it with the hedged mechanism plus the reason: not "the next system over runs on different rules" but "another system may have a different set of rules and no inherent reason to accept it as authoritative." Unpack crux verbs: "without assigning meaning to them" says what "interpreting" means. Precision over playfulness: vivid verbs flatten to mechanism verbs ("compute what it did", not "unwind what it did"; "explores one of them", not "takes one of them and runs it"). Narrowing qualifiers are welcome even at a wordiness cost ("never formally defined"; "the security of an ordering protocol" — attribute properties to the mechanism, not its product). Plain does not mean clipped: keep full ability forms ("does not have the ability to separate", "must retain the ability to").
- **R8. Hedged, institutional nouns over concrete-but-provocative ones.** "The asset itself", not "a token". "Without relying on a designated arbiter" (text posts). "The application state in an arbitrary system". "Execution rules, economic incentives, and governance".
- **R9. Negative definitions say "does not refer to"; don't enumerate what it isn't.** Positive assignment with the term marked in quotes is an allowed alternative ("The research uses the term 'value' to refer to…"); negative enumeration is optional when positive assignment plus context does the work.
- **R10. Hedges mark genuine uncertainty and carry the reason with them.** Never hedge a settled fact; never assert an open one. Hypothetical designs take the conditional ("what a full stack design would need to answer"). No agency verbs for the layer: it carries or does not carry; it does not choose. No totality or exclusivity claims the piece has not established: "part of the work", not "the work"; "a constraint", not "the constraint" — articles must not carry unproven uniqueness. State interdependence between open questions as a relation plus its reason ("depends on both, since…"), never as a verdict on which may be asked first. A scope hedge reports the work done, never its own epistemic status.
- **R11. Make connectives and causal structure explicit.** Add the "however" (mid-clause), spell out "this made it possible", "as a result". Juxtaposed clauses become "Since"-clauses when causality is the point; "so" may strengthen to "and thus". Never rely on juxtaposition to carry the logic.
- **R12. Spell out the implication; no understatement endings.** Close with the explicit consequence ("They cannot be defined by a single organization."), not the dry aphorism ("…or not at all.").
- **R13. Full declaratives, one proposition per sentence; contractions stay.** Split long conditionals into short declaratives. Flatten pseudo-clefts: "Both systems rely on the same thing:…", not "What both systems rely on is…". Formal in structure, not in every word: keep "there's", "doesn't", "and it works".
- **R14. Established terms are the working vocabulary.** Once defined, reuse them; name over gloss or pronoun, especially at the main move. Two exceptions: name-over-pronoun yields when the name already appears in the same sentence (within-sentence repetition reads as redundancy), and a back-reference may dodge a repeated term ("at this level" after "layer"). Never introduce a synonym for an established term, even inside a possessive elision. Mechanism phrases and verbs may repeat verbatim across sibling pieces; the no-repetition rule governs prose texture and framing, not settled mechanism language. Enumerate functions with their objects: "recording what happened, establishing its order, and interpreting its meaning". State a definition once; at the turn, invoke the term — echoing the definition line reads as redundancy even when intended as cohesion.
- **R15. Canonical anchors are paraphrase-proof when present; presence is not mandatory.** "State is always derived from events." — vary the prose around it, never the anchor. Two lengths: cards and renders carry the short form ("State is *always* derived."), prose and captions the full form. The anchor may be embedded as a subordinate causal clause ("Since state is always derived from events, …"). The piece's angle decides which canonical elements appear. For the central claim, the label is fixed and the predicate may vary per piece: "The hypothesis:" / "The working hypothesis:" mid-argument; the standalone label after the claim when the piece has dramatized it first — "That is the working hypothesis." or, naming the owner, "That is OV's working hypothesis."
- **R16. Intermediaries' work is credited, never framed for elimination.** The fee pays for real work, and the work is worth doing well. Open questions target the *duplication* of that work across system boundaries (reconciliation, settlement delay), never the worker, the wage, or the role. Aim the forward question at redo-avoidance: "What would a record have to carry so that this work does not have to be redone at every boundary between systems?"

### 3.3 Confirmed allowances — do not flag

- Direct address (second person).
- Mild intensifiers that set up a real contrast ("works perfectly well within the boundaries of the system, but doesn't necessarily carry across them").
- Concrete example enumerations ("a payment sent, a contract signed, a measurement taken").
- "simply" as a softener inside definitions (2.4).
- One deliberate sentence-starting "But" pivot per piece.
- Schematic refrains as demonstration ("*this* happened / then *this* happened / then *this* happened") — demonstration lines, not fragments.
- A noun-phrase fragment as the direct answer to a "What if" question the piece just posed.
- Canonical term plus plain-language gloss pairing; the gloss fragment may stand as its own sentence.
- Contrastive do-support marking the horns of an open question ("A base that keeps no state… A base that does maintain some state…").
- Bulleted lists for short parallel mechanism enumerations (formatting, not a listicle).
- The X question closers (6) — they are not engagement bait.

## 4. Voice

### 4.1 Where voice comes from

- **What a post says** comes from section 1. **How it sounds** comes from the team's written and spoken register: talks, interviews, standups. These are style exemplars only — never mine them for claims.
- Mimic somewhat, never exactly: pastiche is its own tell. Never lift anyone's sentences. Company posts are never written in anyone's first person.

### 4.2 The moves

Use two or three per post, never all of them.

| Move | When |
|---|---|
| Anchor on infrastructure that already exists (DNS, TCP/IP, ERP, email) | Explaining something unfamiliar. Don't invent new metaphors. |
| State the consequence in the reader's world, not the feature | Any point that could be written as a capability. |
| Ask the question, then answer it | Openings, and turning into the hard part. |
| Give credit before you distinguish | Every comparison. |
| Name the constraint plainly, including the ugly one | Wherever the honest answer is inconvenient. |
| Explain behaviour from first principles | Rarely, and only where the point is about people. |
| A long explanation, then a short flat line | Landing a section. |

Two standing demands: **relevancy** — every post says, inside the post, why it matters to the specific person reading it; and **brevity** — say it in 20 words rather than 80.

### 4.3 Numbers carry their conditions

A number never travels without the condition that produced it.

- Give the range, not the headline figure.
- Put the scope limit inside the claim.
- Say where knowledge stops ("we are not aware of", "that we know of").
- Suggest where others would assert.
- End on the open question, and argue against yourself inside it. This is the model close for every OV post.

### 4.4 Team register

- Sure about the problem, open about the design.
- Internet history rather than crypto vocabulary.
- Credit for everyone, blame for no one.
- One concrete scene and a reason it matters, said fully, then landed in a few words.
- Written for the people who get it; never chase the ones who don't.

### 4.5 Scenes that worked

Use these as models, not as sentences to reuse.

- One payment read twice: the bank reads a settled transfer, a tax authority reads income. Same event, two readings.
- Nobody is ever paid to decide which copy of an email is the real one.
- Email crosses the Internet on protocols nobody owns, while a payment's record stays with the institution that handled it. Value lives on intranets.
- Inside one system, "first" means one clock, one rule for ties, and one authority. Between systems, none of the three is shared.

## 5. Structure

### 5.1 The rotation rule

A piece must differ on at least **two of three axes** — opener, body device, closer — from each of the last **two** pieces on the same platform. Same topic is fine; same shape is not, and matching the piece-before-last (A-B-A) still reads as a repeat. A shape returns to the pool after two intervening pieces. Mechanism phrases may still repeat verbatim (R14); the rotation rule governs structure, not settled language.

### 5.2 Narrative continuity (the two-tier 10-day window)

Consecutive pieces are read in sequence, so each piece must advance the campaign rather than re-run it. Before drafting, scan the past 10 days of pieces across platforms, extending back until at least 6 posting days are covered.

- **Hard window (last 5 days):** a beat stated twice here is spent — compress to mechanism-only or cut.
- **Soft window (days 6–10):** a beat stated twice here is half-spent — reference or compress, never re-state in full. Open questions posed in this range are live threads to advance.
- **The advance requirement:** every piece contributes at least one element the window does not contain — a new question, a new structure, a beat deepened. A piece that only re-states settled beats to keep the account active is filler: flag it, don't publish it.
- Keep a one-line log per published piece (date, platform, opener/body/closer, beats, open question) so threads older than the window survive.

### 5.3 Payload is per-campaign, not per-piece

A beat may compress to mechanism-only when a sibling piece that day carries the full statement; a beat stated twice in the window may be dropped entirely when the piece's advance assumes it. Check the day's sibling pieces before cutting a payload element from a draft.

### 5.4 Body devices (platform-agnostic pool)

Schematic refrain. Concrete-example enumeration. Direct-address scenario. Mechanism enumeration ("three functions: recording…, establishing…, interpreting…"). Definition-by-exclusion. Achievement-then-constraint. Failure-mode confession. Compressed history arc (Internet → intermediaries → blockchains → boundary). Thesis-as-premise statement (naming OV directly). Parallel readings: one event shown through two systems' interpretations side by side, the separation demonstrated rather than stated. Agreement-cost asymmetry: events are cheap to agree on, meaning is expensive, and the cost rises with every system that joins. Adversarial-load enumeration: candidate failure points as pressures in sequence, strict conditional mood. Term decomposition: one word taken apart into the locally-owned components that give it meaning inside one system. Funding-model inventory: each existing way of paying for the security of an ordering shown to answer "who pays" by answering "who owns". Subtraction walk: remove one guarantee from a defined set, trace what survives versus what breaks, name the removed element's cost only after the break is shown. Literature survey: one declarative per lineage with its scope qualifier, the gap stated plainly as a formulation not yet explored.

### 5.5 Platform structure menus

**X (posts and threads):** the argument chain (numbered thread, one inference per post, known problem to open question); the demonstration (show the primitive, don't define it); the caption + anchor (one gloss, one canonical anchor, one labeled hypothesis; pairs with render cards); the reader's problem first; the single question; the inversion (state what accepting another system's record costs, then ask what the record would have to leave out for acceptance to require nothing else); the dependency chain (open questions as interdependent, carried by a conditional chain); the adversarial enumeration (hypothetical-scene opener, pressures in sequence, mid-piece open-question label, compressed closing label); the word anatomy (the piece's term declared system-local, decomposed into its locally-owned components, closed on a bare question); the unpaid invoice (funding-model inventory, each model's owner named, the neutral-layer hypothetical facing the same bill); the subtraction test (definition-list opener as colon glosses, one guarantee removed, close on the bare research question plus reader question).

**LinkedIn:** the concept-pair explainer; the term-precision post; the steelman (credit what came before exactly, locate the constraint it left, close with failure-mode honesty — the safe mapping of the contrarian take); the reader-cost post; the open-question post; the vehicle inventory (one declarative per concrete object, the definition emerging inductively); the thesis walk (unpack a paired render card beat by beat in the order result → design → inheritance; the sequence is the device — no visible signposts, verbatim quoting not required); the two readings (worked demonstration, one event read by two systems); the process trace (strict chronological order, one declarative per stage, terms folded in at the stage where they apply, deadline as a standalone line); the literature survey (gap statement, scope hedge reported as work done).

**YouTube Shorts (conversational register; hooks required):** negate-the-misconception; the big question (largest form, answered through a compressed history arc); the concrete scene; the common assumption ("Most systems assume X. The part that assumption misses is…"); the cost-arc (reframe the cost of resolving disagreement as wages for real labor, credited per R16); the crossing trace (one explicitly marked example event traced across the boundary in strict chronological order, the second system's choice stated as a fork: derive its own answer from the events, or redo the work). Closers rotate independently of the hook: pointer to the full piece, series pointer, the deeper question the series answers, or no pointer at all.

### 5.6 Banned shapes

Tip/mistake listicles ("5 mistakes you're making…"); hot takes; before/after transformation posts; engagement bait ("Agree?", "Repost if…", any empty prompt); LinkedIn one-line-per-sentence padding. Enumeration of *mechanism* is doctrine and stays; enumeration of *tips* is guru-format and is out.

### 5.7 What never rotates

Register rules (R1–R16), canonical terms, anchors when present, classification honesty, no hype. Structural novelty never licenses register novelty: do not reach for hype structures for the sake of variety.

## 6. Closing lines and questions

- Close each post in one of three ways: on the open question, arguing against yourself; on a question aimed at a **named kind of reader** ("If you run reconciliation for a living…"); or on "Join the research."
- **One question per post**, never two. It must be answerable from the reader's own experience. "What do you think?", "Agree?", and polls are all banned (LinkedIn polls also stopped performing in March 2026).
- Each Monday post answers its own question with a stated guess the reader can push back on.
- **CTA:** "Join the research." — LinkedIn and video only. It never appears on X; Substack Notes needs no CTA.
- **X closers, three settled forms:** (a) a reader question carrying the piece's open question ("Which would you expect to break first?"); (b) the piece's own open question asked bare, no label and no addressee — the question form is its own classification; (c) both sharing the close, labeled research question first and reader question as the exit, with the label allowed postposed ("Could a base layer offer that much, or only the first two? That remains an open question."). A reply-pointer line may follow the question.
- **Video closers:** the standing closer may be dropped when the piece closes on a forward open question. Series pointers are optional and never framed mid-piece.

## 7. Cadence and formats

### 7.1 Weekly rhythm

| Day | Slot | Form |
|---|---|---|
| Mon | Engagement | A short post built around one question for a named reader. LinkedIn: 400 to 700 characters. X: a single post under 280 characters. |
| Tue | Long form | LinkedIn long post or document carousel, plus an X thread. |
| Wed | Media | The 30s reel on YouTube Shorts, LinkedIn, and X, each with its own caption. Also GIFs and cards. |
| Thu | Long form | Same as Tuesday. |
| Fri to Sun | Nothing new | Replies only. |

- One idea per day, written natively for each platform.
- Thesis content only until the research program is public (1.6).

### 7.2 Platform specifics

From the algorithm studies dated 9 September, which are due for a refresh.

**LinkedIn**
- Document carousels are the strongest format, about 6.6% engagement, best at 5 to 10 slides.
  - Each slide under about 30 words.
  - Slide 1 is a question or a scene.
  - The last slide is the open question plus the CTA.
  - The caption runs 300 to 600 characters.
- Long posts: **1,200 to 1,700 characters** for dwell time.
- The point must land in the first 140 characters, before "see more".
- **Links:** none in the body and none in the first comment (the first-comment workaround is now penalised too). Links live in the newsletter and the page profile.
- The first 60 minutes decide distribution, so the team leaves substantive comments in that first hour.
- Post Tuesday to Thursday, 8 to 10am ET, no more than 4 or 5 page posts a week.

**X**
- A reply is worth about 27 likes, more when the author answers it; a bookmark about 10.
- Engagement in the first 15 minutes counts most.
- **Threads:** 4 to 7 posts, each under 280 characters; post 1 must stand alone. Threads get about 3x the engagement of single posts. Threads and long single posts are both first-class; choose by the piece's shape, and let the rotation rule (5.1) arbitrate over time.
- Links go in a reply, never in the post.
- A card post is 180 to 320 characters. A card compresses something already reviewed and never makes a new claim.
- At most 4 images per post.

**YouTube Shorts and reels**
- 1080x1920, 30 to 60 seconds, captions always on (91% of LinkedIn video is watched muted).
- Keep text out of the top 12% and bottom 20% of the frame.
- The title is a neutral question under 40 characters, never clickbait.
- The first two lines of the description carry the point. The last line asks the open question, never a summary.
- Banked reel scripts render as written apart from the mechanics in 2.5; the captions written for each platform go through this file in full.

**Substack Notes**
- 1 to 4 sentences, first person plural, no CTA.
